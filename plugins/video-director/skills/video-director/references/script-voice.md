# How a script sounds

`craft.md` §8 says what goes where: hook, intrigue, delivery, exit. This file says how the lines sound. Read it
before writing any script line, and write every line by following one of the patterns below, by name.

**Why this file exists.** The four-part order on its own produces scripts that are in the right order but sound
like an advert or a machine: abstract claims, lists of benefits, lines that don't lead into each other, nothing
human in them. The order is the frame; these patterns are the voice.

**About the examples.** Every example line here is made up to show a pattern (a café owner, a plumber, a
bookkeeper, a dog groomer). Never reuse them. Follow the pattern and write it in the person's own words, from
their own sources.

---

## 1. Length and rhythm

- **One thought, 40–60 seconds.** If a second idea turns up, it's a second video.
- **About 105–150 words.** People talk to camera at about 2.4 words a second (130–160 a minute), so 45 s is
  about 105 words and 60 s about 145. A 60-second draft over about 150 words has a second thought in it.
- **Short sentences**, mostly under 12 words. A story can open on one longer breath; after that the sentences
  shorten.
- **One or two sentences per shot** (craft §8), so the speaker only holds that much in their head.
- **Longer pieces only when the person asks for one:** several of these units chained around the same one idea,
  with a 40–60 s version cut from the same footage.

## 2. The opening (hook)

Start on the first useful word. No hello, no name, no "welcome back" (craft §9). Use one of these:

- **Name who it's for.** The first line says who this is for and the moment they're in, so the right viewer
  knows in two seconds that it's theirs.
  *Example:* "If you run a café and the morning rush swallows your whole day, this one's for you."
- **Invite, then "because".** Offer something to try in a few words. The next line starts with "Because" and
  names a problem the viewer already has, in their words.
  *Example:* "Give this a go tonight. / Because every quote you send at 10 pm is a quote you rushed."
- **Back then.** Open on the speaker's earlier self, with a time stamp and one concrete detail of being stuck,
  in a single breath. Then the turn: "And then…" or "Now…".
  *Example:* "Three years ago I was doing the books at the kitchen table at midnight, with the kids' homework
  pushed to one end."
- **Their moment, back to them.** Describe the viewer's exact situation with a number, then gently doubt the
  advice they keep getting.
  *Example:* "You posted every day last month and about a dozen people saw each one. Everyone says post more.
  I'm not sure that's the fix."

Whichever you use:
- **A promise or a problem they recognise, never a riddle.** A clever, vague opener tells the viewer nothing
  about what they'll get, so they leave.
- **The line after the hook holds back the answer.** It gives the reason this matters, or the price of ignoring
  it. If line 2 gives the answer away, the viewer has what they came for and scrolls on.

## 3. How lines connect

- **Start most sentences with a spoken joiner:** Because, And, So, But, Then, Now. "Because" carries the hook
  into the intrigue. "So" turns a problem into the move. "But" brings the turn in a story. "And then" or "Now"
  marks the change. Never a written joiner ("Furthermore", "Additionally", "Moreover").
  *Example:* "Most leaks you'll never see. / So read your water meter tonight, last thing. / And read it again
  before anyone's up. / If it moved, you've found one."
- **Show it while you say it.** The video does the thing it's talking about, and the line points at the
  picture. Every line then has a shot.
  *Example:* "See the number on the dial? Write that down."
- **Say the objection out loud.** Agree with the feeling first, then answer it in a few words.
  *Example:* "I know. One more job on the list. But it's two minutes, and it saves the Monday panic."
- **Shuffle test.** If a line could move anywhere in the script and nothing would break, it isn't connected.
  Put a joiner in front of it, even one you won't say, and check it still follows the line before.

## 4. Where warmth comes from

- **Admit something first.** The speaker's own mistake comes before the lesson. An admission does more for
  warmth than any compliment to the viewer.
  *Example:* "I priced my first fifty jobs by guessing. Badly."
- **Beside the viewer, not above them.** Invite rather than instruct. Use "we" for a struggle you share. Never
  tell the viewer off or lump them into a crowd that gets it wrong.
- **Balance "you" and "I".** A tip piece is mostly "you", with one "I" admission. A story piece is mostly "I" or
  "we", then turns to "you" in the last line or two. All "I" with no turn reads as a brag; all "you" with no "I"
  reads as a lecture.
- **Plain, casual words**, and soften big claims ("I think", "for me", "it might").
- **Stakes in time and people, not money.** If money comes up, it comes second to an evening back, being at
  school pickup, a weekend off.
- **A small joke or play on words** can close a point. Never at the viewer's expense.

## 5. Concreteness

- **Every claim gets something you can picture:** an object, an exact small number, or a moment. Not "save
  time" but "home by six on a Friday". Not "grow your business" but "three new regulars this month".
- **Credentials as a scene, once, early**, never a list of titles.
  *Example:* "I've crawled under about four hundred kitchen sinks."
- **Film test.** If you can't say what the shot is for a line, the line is abstract. Rewrite it around a thing,
  a number or a moment.

## 6. Endings (exit)

Always short. Use one of these:

- **A tag question** that hands the confidence back to the viewer.
  *Example:* "Two minutes tonight. That's doable, isn't it?"
- **One small thing to do today, asked as a question.**
  *Example:* "So which of your prices haven't you touched in two years? Pick one and look at it tonight."
- **A plain follow ask at the very end**, tied to the topic and framed as company, not a favour. Never at the
  start.
  *Example:* "Follow along. Next week I'll show you the price list I use."
- **A question about their own experience**, for the comments.
  *Example:* "Which job do you still undercharge for? Tell me below."
- **A loop.** End on a half-sentence that runs straight into the first line when the video replays.
  *Example:* first line "Read your water meter tonight."; last line "So if you're not sure where to start…"
- **The person's own sign-off line**, if they have one they use every time. Never borrow someone else's.

## 7. How an offer is mentioned

- **Once, late, in passing.** One line, two-thirds of the way through or near the end, as the easy version of
  what was just shown.
  *Example:* "And if you'd rather not build the checklist yourself, mine's free. Link's below."
- **Name a product once, as the easy option.** Never describe its features.
- **Never in the hook, and never urgent** (craft §13).

## 8. What it is NOT (the voice check)

Before building the script page, read every line against this list. Rewrite each hit by following a pattern
above, not by swapping words.

- **Abstract triads:** "clarity, consistency, confidence"; "the right time, the right place, the right
  people". Each claim needs an object, a number or a moment (§5).
- **A list of benefits:** save time, reduce stress, grow faster. One thought, shown.
- **Product talk:** what a tool does, how it works, its features. At most one product named, once (§7).
- **"I built…" or "I created…" as the point.** The maker's story isn't the viewer's problem.
- **Stock openers:** "Hey guys", a name-and-channel intro, "Here's the thing", "Let's dive in", "Did you know",
  "Imagine if", "What if I told you", "Stop scrolling".
- **Sales words:** game-changer, unlock, transform, seamless, effortless, "don't miss out".
- **Lecturing:** "you need to", "you should", telling the viewer they're doing it wrong (craft §9).
- **Several tips.** Two ideas are two videos.
- **A riddle hook** that promises nothing, or **the answer in line 2** (§2).
- **Written prose:** "Furthermore", long noun phrases, unexplained jargon, em dashes, "not X, it's Y" as a
  habit, "quietly" (concept.md §3).
- **Brag numbers as the payoff.** Numbers are context; time and people are the payoff (§4).
- **Lines with no joiner** that fail the shuffle test (§3).
- **Over about 150 words for 60 seconds** (§1).

## 9. Three templates (structure only, 45–60 s, about 105–150 words)

Pick one before writing. Each step is one or two sentences unless it says otherwise.

### A. Something to try
1. **Hook:** name who it's for, or invite them to try something.
2. **Intrigue:** "Because…" and the problem they already feel, in their words. Not the answer.
3. **Delivery:** "So…" and the one move, shown on screen as it's said; if it helps, the usual way first, then the
   better way. (two or three short lines, each its own shot)
4. **Delivery:** the objection said out loud, answered in one line.
5. **Delivery:** why it works, in how it feels, not in technical terms.
6. **Exit:** a tag question or a small joke.
7. **Exit:** a follow ask tied to the topic, or a half-sentence that loops back to line 1.

### B. Then and now
1. **Hook:** the speaker's earlier self, with a time stamp and one concrete detail of being stuck (one longer
   breath, about 20–35 words).
2. **Intrigue:** the low point made physical: a place, an object, a person it affected.
3. **Delivery:** the turn ("And then…" / "Now…") and the one thing they started doing.
4. **Delivery:** what it bought them, in time, people or freedom; any number comes second.
5. **Delivery:** an admission that keeps it honest: it was hard, it took a while, still learning.
6. **Exit:** turn to "you" and name who this is for, warmly and specifically.
7. **Exit:** the person's own sign-off, or an invitation to follow along.

### C. Their moment
1. **Hook:** the viewer's exact situation, with a number or a moment.
2. **Intrigue:** the advice they keep hearing, and a gentle doubt about it.
3. **Delivery:** the speaker's own version of the same mistake, briefly.
4. **Delivery:** the one different way to see it, said plainly and tied to an object or image.
5. **Exit:** the single small thing to do today.
6. **Exit:** a tag question, or a question about their experience.

## 10. Writing and checking a script

1. Pick a template (§9).
2. Write each line by following a named pattern in this file. If no pattern fits a line, it probably doesn't
   belong in the video.
3. Record each line's pattern in the script file's **Pattern** column. It's a working note for you: never put
   it on the person's page, where it reads as jargon.
4. Count the words and divide by 2.4 for the spoken seconds (§1).
5. Run the voice check (§8) and the shuffle test (§3). Read every line aloud.
6. Then the label audit and the page (concept.md §4).
